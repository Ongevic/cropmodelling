Abstract
Industrial hemp ( Cannabis sativa L.) is a re-emerging crop in the United States
with unique agronomic challenges that require location-specific studies and guid-
ance. Digital farming tools, such as crop growth models, can facilitate this process by
enabling a better understanding of the farming system. Crop growth models predict
the growth and development of crops over time using weather, soil, management,
and physiological parameters as inputs. The goal of this study was to develop a
new hemp model in the Cropping System Model (CSM)-CROPGRO module in the
Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT). Experimental data
spanning two cultivars, both grown over two seasons and two sites in Florida, were
used for model calibration and evaluation. Model adaptations were made in (1) tis-
sue composition and assimilate partitioning, (2) cardinal temperatures for different
growth and development processes, and (3) leaf photosynthesis and senescence. The
results show a good simulation of aboveground biomass (d= 0.91, root mean square
error [RMSE] = 482 kg ha−1), stem weight (d = 0.83, RMSE = 430 kg ha−1), and
time to flowering ( +4t o −5 days), capturing the differences among cultivars and
planting dates. A seasonal analysis using the adapted model showed the impact of
variable planting dates on hemp phenology, biomass, and grain production. Future

Plain Language Summary
Industrial hemp is a crop grown for fiber and seed, with renewed interest in the United
States due to its many uses and sustainable benefits. This study developed and tested
a new computer model to predict how hemp grows and produces fiber and seed in
Florida. The model helps farmers and researchers understand how different planting
dates, locations, and plant types affect hemp growth. The model accurately predicted
hemp growth, flowering time, and yield using field data. It showed that planting hemp
around June 1 in northern Florida gives the highest fiber yield, due to the plant’s
response to day length. The model also found that longer daylight or special hemp
types can greatly increase productivity. The new hemp model is a valuable tool for
planning and improving hemp farming in Florida and beyond. Choosing the right
planting date and hemp type is crucial for best production. The model will help guide
future research and farming decisions.
1 INTRODUCTION
Industrial hemp ( Cannabis sativa L.) is an annual dioecious
plant with versatile uses for fiber, seed, oil, and medicinal pur-
poses. It belongs to the family Cannabaceae, along with hops
(Humulus lupulus L.) and hackberries (Celtis spp.). Hemp has
a long history of cultivation and use, dating back as far as
8000 years ago in China, India, and the Middle East (Small,
2015). It is considered a more sustainable and water-efficient
alternative to cotton (Gossypium L.) fiber (Schumacher et al.,
2020; Wise et al., 2023) and has use in various industrial
applications from manufacturing to construction (Ranalli &
Venturi, 2004; Rehman et al., 2021; Van der Werf et al.,
1996). Given recent policy changes and the legalization of the
crop, hemp has found renewed interest, especially in North
America. Subsequently, several regional research programs
were formed (Cherney & Small, 2016; Mylavarapu et al.,
2020) to study production and economics of hemp, but also
its social-ecological aspects such as invasion risk and con-
sumer perception (Canavan et al.,2022; Rampold et al.,2021).
The open-field acreage for fiber, seed and grain hemp in
2023 in the United States totaled 7056 ha and had a value
of $16.82 million, a significant increase over 2022 (USDA
NASS, 2024). Despite the wide range of uses and potential
markets (Expert Market Research, 2024; G. Kaur & Kander,
2023), multiple studies highlight the current limited knowl-
edge and research needs on hemp production and economics
(Adesina et al., 2020; Mark et al., 2020).
On the agronomic side, understanding the variability and
interaction of genetic, environmental, and management fac-
tors is a critical step when introducing a new crop to
identify or develop locally adapted cultivars and manage-
ment approaches (Hatfield & Walthall, 2015). Given the
complexity of these interactions, data science and process-
based crop growth modeling are approaches to advance our
understanding of the hemp crop and the surrounding agricul-
ture system (Jones et al., 2017; Tsuji et al., 1998). A crop
growth model simulates the growth and development of a crop
and connected soil–plant–atmosphere processes, such as the
plant and soil water and nutrient balances, based on underly-
ing physical and biological relations (Jones et al., 2003). A
process-based model goes beyond empirical correlations by
defining a mechanistic relationships of underlying processes
driving plant growth (Cuddington et al.,2013; Spitters,1990).
It is to be differentiated from purely data-driven models or
artificial intelligence in data requirements, incorporation of
biophysical knowledge, and explainability of results, although
recent work combines advantages from both approaches
(Maestrini et al., 2022; Worrall et al., 2023; N. Zhang
et al., 2023).

Crop growth models and DSS, such as the Decision Support
System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT, Hoogenboom
et al., 2019, 2024 ; Jones et al., 2003), are often high-
lighted for their applications in precision agriculture (Thorp

increase outputs while reducing inputs (Aubert et al., 2012).
Therefore, developing a process-based model for simulat-
ing the growth and development of industrial hemp can
be a significant contribution to advancing our understand-
ing of the hemp plant and developing sustainable cropping
systems.
Previous models simulating the growth and development
of hemp have been developed or adapted for selected fiber
and seed cultivars grown in different environments, build-
ing upon different physiological modeling approaches. In
one of the first hemp model studies, Van der Werf et al.
(1999) simulated hemp production potential in Europe using
the “Light Interception and Utilization” model (Spitters et
al., 1989) with temperature-dependent development and dry
matter production and partitioning based on the concepts of
light interception and radiation use efficiency (RUE). Lisson
et al. ( 2000) modeled different fiber hemp cultivars in Aus-
tralia with the “Agricultural Production Systems sIMulator”
model (Keating et al.,2003). Phenology was modeled using a
growing degree day approach with an empirically determined
base temperature of 1˚C and optimum temperature of 29˚C
and the biomass accumulation was based on an RUE coeffi-
cient multiplied by intercepted radiation. Intercepted radiation
was calculated using Beer’s law based on incident photosyn-
thetically active radiation (PAR), an extinction coefficient,
and the leaf area index (LAI). Total LAI was simulated by
coupling individual processes for leaf appearance, leaf senes-
cence, and leaf expansion as functions of thermal time. A
recently published study by Žydelis et al. ( 2022) adapted the
AgroC model (Klosterhalfen et al., 2017) for hemp, which
uses SUCROS (Spitters et al.,1989)f o rt h ec r o pg r o w t hc o m -

if applicable. In another approach based on the “AquaCrop”

water-focused model. Hence, photosynthesis is not included

type of model user, among other factors (K. J. Boote et al.,

Our review of these previous modeling activities on indus-
trial hemp concludes that many models and underlying
datasets are not publicly available, have not been calibrated
with data from North America, and are not built with a
specific focus on evaluating agronomic management, includ-
ing the accessibility of the model to non-technical users.
Therefore, to advance and complement previous modeling
activities, the overall goal of this research project was to
develop a process-based model for hemp based on the Crop-
ping System Model (CSM) of DSSAT for field applications.

2 MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1 Experimental data
Agronomic hemp trials were conducted in 2021 and 2022 at
multiple sites across Florida under the auspices of the Univer-
sity of Florida’s Industrial Hemp Program ( https://programs.
ifas.ufl.edu/hemp). These trials included different planting
dates, fertilization rates, and cultivars. Initial model devel-
opment was based on two sites: the Plant Science Research
and Education Unit (PSREU,https://plantscienceunit.ifas.ufl.
edu) near Citra in north-central Florida (29.409˚ N,−82.171˚
W) with Arredondo sand soil series and the West Florida
Research and Education Center (WFREC, https://wfrec.ifas.
ufl.edu) near Jay in north-western Florida (30.777˚ N, 87.142˚
W) with Dothan fine sandy loam soil series. The full
experimental setup, including a description of pre-plant soil
characteristics, measurement methodology, and results for
other hemp trials at these sites that were not included in the
model development process, can be found in the respective
publication for the PSREU site (N. Kaur et al., 2024) and the
WFREC site (Nwosu et al., 2025). To the extent possible,
experimental data collection was conducted in accordance
with standards for crop modeling (K. J. Boote et al., 2016;
White et al., 2013) and the recently established industry
and research standards for industrial hemp physiology and
morphology (Brym et al., 2023).
The trials used for model adaptation included the fiber cul-
tivar IH Williams (IND Hemp, also known as SS-Beta) and
the multi-purpose cultivar NWG 2730 (New West Genetics).
Trials were laid out in a randomized complete block design
with six nitrogen (N) fertilizer rates (0, 56, 112, 168, 224, and
280 kg N ha−1) in the form of urea as treatments. The plots
were 7.62 m long and 3.6 m wide with 0.2 m planting row
spacing and 2 m or wider alleys between plots. Seeds were
direct sown with a live seed rate of 300,000–544,500 ha −1 at
different dates in May of each year depending on weather and
seed availability. At the PSREU site, fertilizer was applied in
split applications with 20% at planting, 30% at 20 days after
planting, and 50% at 40 days after planting. The WFREC
site had an initial application of 56 kg N ha −1 at 10 days
after planting followed by the remaining N amount at circa 35
days after planting using a drop spreader (GDY-6506TR-PPL,
Gandy Company). Overhead irrigation, from a central pivot
system at site PSREU and lateral move system at site WFREC,
was applied in one or more doses of 13–19 mm during the
first 45 days after sowing to improve plant establishment and
incorporate N. At the WFREC site, weed control was con-
ducted pre-planting with 1.4 L ha−1 grass and broadleaf weed
herbicide (Dual Magnum, Syngenta) and post-planting with
a cultivator or manual removal. The PSREU site only con-
ducted post-planting weed control with a cultivator or manual
removal.
Among the various trials conducted at these sites, only
the best-performing experiments with minimal weed pressure,
an acceptable germination rate, and good crop performance
were chosen for model adaptation and calibration. A total of
15 combinations of different site, year, cultivar, and fertil-
ization combinations were selected as they performed well
in the field trials and had the most observations, and they
are described in more detail in the Section “ 2.4 Simula-
tion Settings and Inputs” and Table 2. However, compared
to other locations and particularly sites in Europe or China
with a longer cultivation history, total dry biomass productiv-
ity was generally lower due to a shortened vegetative growth
phase and early flowering as well as general challenges with
seed quality and plant establishment. Measurements for total
biomass, tissue partitioning, number of nodes on main stem,
plant height, and maximum plant width were obtained multi-
ple times throughout the season with three or more repetitions
per treatment. For each biomass measurement, plants from
a representative area of circa 0.25 m × 0.25 m in the early
growth stages increasing to 1.0 m× 1.0 m in the later growth
stages with increasing plant size were harvested and analyzed.
Plant height, width, and number of nodes were measured on
the removed plants and at additional dates throughout the
season on standing plants without harvesting them. Addi-
tionally, phenological observations were recorded for some
experiments, including emergence, beginning flowering (first
terminal flower), full flowering (50% of male plants termi-
nal flowering), start of seed formation, and when the majority
of seeds were mature. Terminal flowering is defined as the
first defined clusters of florets at the shoot apex following
Brym et al. ( 2023). Missing observations for terminal flow-
ering were estimated as 10 days before 50% of male plants
flowering based on a time series of phenological observa-
tions at other sites. Travel distance limited the number of
field visits possible, leading to a varying number of mea-
surements for some experiments. A high degree of variability
was found between blocks of the same treatment, likely
due to within-field variations and weed pressure in some
plots. Besides the specific field experiment described, more
general knowledge on hemp physiology, phenology, and man-
agement response derived from the remaining sites of the
University of Florida’s Industrial Hemp Program was also
considered during the model development and adaptation
process.

2.2 Weather data
The weather data for the two experimental locations were
obtained from the Florida Automated Weather Network
(FAWN, Lusher et al., 2008). The station “Citra” (29.410˚
N 82.173˚ W) was installed in 2003 and is located near the
PSREU field trials. The station “Jay” (30.775˚ N 87.141˚
W) was installed in 2003 and is located near the WFREC
field trials. Both stations provide daily minimum and maxi-
mum air temperature, daily total precipitation, daily total solar
radiation, wind speed, and relative humidity, in addition to
more detailed measurements (Table 1). Both field sites are
characterized by a humid subtropical climate with a rainy
season from May through October. The day length, which
is relevant for the simulation of photoperiod and phenology,
is calculated based on solar declination, latitude, and longi-
tude of the station. To evaluate the simulation of flowering
dates for sites outside of Florida, satellite-derived weather
data from NASA-Power (NASA, 2018) were obtained for
Urbana, IL (40.114˚ N, 88.234˚ W); Hyde, MI (45.734˚
N, 87.203˚ W); and Manhattan, KS (39.184˚ N, 96.572˚
W). The sites have phenology observations in the Midwest-
ern Hemp Database (Alberti, 2021; University of Illinois,
2024) for the same cultivars but at different latitudes which
makes them suitable for the evaluation of the phenology
simulation.
2.3 Software, source code, and statistics
The crop simulations were performed with the CSM model
(Jones et al., 2003) in the modeling software DSSAT 4.8.2
(Hoogenboom et al., 2019, 2024) available at http://www.
dssat.net. The source code is available via a GitHub repository
at https://github.com/DSSAT/dssat-csm-os. DSSAT encom-
passes a wide range of models and groups of models, with
overarching data infrastructure, graphical user interface, and
support functions. CROPGRO is one specific group of mod-
els within the CSM and was the framework for the hemp
model development. Model parameters changed during the
model development for hemp will be presented in Section
“ 3.1 Model Development Process.” The full set of param-
eters and required files for CSM–CROPGRO-Hemp, later
referred to as CROPGRO-Hemp, are provided as Supporting
Information 1 and will be implemented in a future version
of DSSAT for public release. Statistical analysis and visual-
ization of the results were performed in R-Studio 2023.06.2
(RStudio Team, 2020) and R 3.4.2 (R Core Team, 2021).

2.4 Simulation settings and inputs
Crop model input and simulation files were created for each
individual experiment to resemble the field trials and sim-
ulate growth and development under both zero N input and
high N fertilizer applications (Table2). The maximum rate of
280 kg N ha−1 applied in the experiments is at the upper end,
if not beyond, the recommended N fertilization range iden-
tified in the literature (N. Kaur et al., 2023). This treatment
was selected for the development of the model to simulate
a scenario close to potential production that is limited only
by the weather conditions. In selected experiments, a lower
N rate was chosen for the simulation setup because more in-
season observations were available for the lower treatment
or because high N rates performed worse than moderate N
rates. The underlying adverse external effects or irregularities
in local field conditions would be challenging to quantify and
adequately represent in an initial model version. Due to vary-
ing seed emergence rates and thinning during the first weeks
of crop establishment, the observed plant stands were signif-
icantly below the targeted plant density. Since self-thinning
is not yet included in the CROPGRO model framework, the
planting density for modeling purposes was based on the stand
count that was obtained at 4 weeks after the first emergence,
assuming that these plants would survive until the end of
the season. The simulation of potassium (K) and phosphorus
(P) dynamics and N-fixing bacteria symbiosis was deacti-
vated in the simulation settings because of a lack of suitable
data for calibration and because it is not applicable to hemp
as a nonlegume crop, although the DSSAT framework gen-
erally supports these capabilities. Enabled by the modular
structure of DSSAT and CROPGRO, several submodules are
available to simulate the same biophysical and biochemical
processes based on different scientific principles or assump-
tions. The “CENTURY” module (Gijsman et al., 2002)w a s
chosen for soil organic matter, the “Ritchie Water Balance”
(Ritchie, 1998) for hydrology, the “Soil Conservation Series”
for infiltration, the “Ritchie-Ceres” for soil evaporation, the
“Modified Soil Profile” for soil horizon distribution, and
“Priestley-Taylor” for potential evapotranspiration (Hoogen-
boom et al., 2019). The choice of individual submodules was
based on the CROPGRO-Soybean ( Glycine max ) template
and was deemed feasible given the experimental data that
were available and the modeling results that were obtained.
Different future research questions, locations, and experi-
mental data might necessitate an evaluation of alternative
modules. To evaluate the simulation of flowering dates for
sites outside of Florida, simplified simulations were set up
with deactivated water and N dynamics and standard soil pro-
file and management information, since only the phenology
simulation was of interest.
2.5 Soil data
Soil profile information (Table 3) was obtained by measure-
ments and estimated based on comparison to soil profiles from
previous crop modeling studies and the soil series descrip-
tion for a Dothan fine sandy loam at WFREC (K. J. Boote
et al., 2021; USDA NRCS, 2017) and for an Arredondo
sand at PSREU (de Souza Nóia Júnior et al., 2022;U S D A
NRCS, 2018). The stable soil organic carbon pool was man-
ually determined throughout the model calibration process to

2.6 Model adaptation and calibration
process
The existing models for soybean and cotton of CSM-
CROPGRO were used as a template, as they are well-studied
crops with extensive model development, applications, and
supporting experimental data for the model parameters (K.
Boote et al., 1997; Hoogenboom et al., 1992;L ie ta l . ,2019;
Thorp et al., 2014). The CROPGRO parameters for soy-
bean and cotton, therefore, served as reference values to
which hemp was compared. Furthermore, both the grain and
fiber aspects of hemp production are relevant aspects in this
study, which have similarities with both soybean and cotton
production systems. CROPGRO is a generic modeling tem-
plate within DSSAT, initially developed for grain legumes
with hourly leaf-level photosynthesis calculations coupled
with hedgerow-type light interception (K. Boote & Pickering,
1994). Additionally, it contains soil–plant–nitrogen and water
balance as well as photosynthetic, vegetative, and reproduc-
tive growth and development processes (K. J. Boote, Jones, &
Hoogenboom, 1998; K. J. Boote, Jones, Hoogenboom, et al.,
1998). Physiological and genetic parameters in CROPGRO
describe the complex traits of the crop, separated into species,
ecotype, and cultivar parameters. The hierarchical organiza-
tion aims to provide a universal foundation for a crop (species
parameters), which is then further customized for distinct
groups of ecotypes and individual cultivars. The template and
parameters for the species, ecotype, and cultivar files were
adjusted following the example of recent adaptations of the
CROPGRO model for guar ( Cyamopsis tetragonoloba;K .J .
Boote et al., 2023), carinata ( Brassica carinata;K .J .B o o t e
et al., 2021), strawberry ( Fragaria× ananassa; Hopf et al.,
2022), safflower ( Carthamus tinctorius ; Singh et al., 2016),
and alfalfa (Medicago sativa; Malik et al., 2018). The overall
process was predominantly manual, with iterative adjustment
of parameters and comparison between simulated model out-
puts and observed data (Figure 1). Whereas some parameters
were clearly defined through experimental evidence or litera-
ture review, other parameters were estimated from a feasible
range of values across other crop species of CSM-CROPGRO.
The optimization tools GLUE and GENCALC (He et al.,
2010; Hunt et al.,1993; Jones et al.,2011) included in DSSAT
were used to a limited extent for the calibration of the cultivar
parameters for phenology.
